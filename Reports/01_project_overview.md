# Project Overview: Underwater Vessel Noise Classification

## 1. Dataset Context

The foundational data source for this underwater vessel noise classification system is the ShipsEar dataset, a comprehensive and publicly available repository of underwater acoustic recordings. Created and curated by Santos-Domínguez et al. (2016), the dataset was specifically designed to facilitate research in passive acoustic monitoring and automatic classification of marine acoustic signatures. The objective of the dataset is to provide a robust corpus of real-world hydroacoustic data capturing the radiated noise of various vessel types under operational conditions.

The collection of the ShipsEar dataset was conducted in authentic maritime environments, specifically along the Spanish Atlantic coast and the port of Vigo, utilizing bottom-moored hydrophones. The recordings capture the radiated acoustic emissions of passing vessels under varying weather and ambient noise conditions, ensuring that the dataset represents a realistic cross-section of the acoustic challenges encountered in littoral zones. These challenges include multipath propagation, varying bathymetry, surface wave noise, and biological interference. The hydrophone arrays were calibrated and positioned strategically to capture both the broadband noise generated by propeller cavitation and the narrowband tonal components arising from onboard machinery such as diesel engines and generators.

Quantitatively, the ShipsEar dataset comprises 90 individual acoustic recordings, yielding a total duration of approximately three hours of high-fidelity underwater sound. The audio signals were sampled at a high rate of 52,734 Hz to capture a wide spectrum of acoustic phenomena, encompassing both low-frequency structural vibrations and high-frequency cavitation noise. This high-resolution temporal data allows for detailed spectral and cepstral analysis.

For the purposes of classification in this project, the dataset is stratified into five distinct classes based on their acoustic signatures and operational characteristics:
1.  **CargoShip:** Large commercial cargo vessels designed for transporting goods, generating significant continuous broadband noise and low-frequency tonals due to heavy propulsion systems.
2.  **KaiYuan:** Naval vessels of the KaiYuan class, which present unique acoustic profiles often distinct from civilian commercial vessels due to specialized hull designs and machinery.
3.  **noise:** Ambient underwater noise serving as a negative control class. This includes natural environmental sounds (waves, wind, marine life) without prominent, nearby vessel acoustic signatures.
4.  **SpeedBoat:** High-speed small watercraft characterized by high-RPM engines, producing prominent high-frequency cavitation and distinct acoustic bursts corresponding to rapid maneuvering.
5.  **Uuv:** Unmanned Underwater Vehicles (UUVs). These submersibles typically use quiet electric thrusters, creating a very subtle, localized, and highly specific tonal acoustic signature compared to large surface vessels.

These five classes present a challenging multi-class classification problem due to intra-class variability (e.g., different ships within the same class) and inter-class similarities under variable oceanic propagation conditions.

## 2. Acoustic Theory

The development of an effective underwater acoustic classification system requires a rigorous understanding of how sound behaves in a marine environment, which contrasts significantly with atmospheric acoustics. In the ocean, sound is the primary modality for long-range propagation due to the rapid attenuation of electromagnetic waves in saline water. The speed of sound underwater is nominally 1500 meters per second—approximately 4.5 times faster than in air—and is a complex function of temperature, salinity, and depth (pressure). This creates a highly dispersive and refractive medium. Furthermore, shallow water environments, such as those where the ShipsEar dataset was collected, act as acoustic waveguides bounded by the sea surface and the ocean floor. This physical bounding leads to severe multipath propagation, reverberation, and frequency-selective fading, substantially complicating signal analysis.

The system relies entirely on the principles of passive sonar (Sound Navigation and Ranging). Unlike active sonar, which emits acoustic pulses and analyzes the resulting echoes, passive sonar systems are strictly interceptive. They utilize hydrophones to listen to the ambient acoustic field and detect the radiated noise of targets of interest without revealing the sensor's own location. The acoustic signature of a vessel is a complex composite signal consisting of continuous broadband noise (primarily originating from propeller cavitation and hydrodynamic flow around the hull) and discrete narrowband frequencies (tonals) associated with the rotational frequencies of the main engine, reduction gears, pumps, and other internal machinery.

Each class in the utilized dataset exhibits distinct frequency characteristics governed by their physical dimensions, displacement, and propulsion mechanisms. Large vessels, such as CargoShips and KaiYuan naval vessels, typically dominate the low-frequency spectrum (below 1000 Hz) with high-amplitude tonals corresponding to massive engines and large-diameter propellers. Conversely, smaller vessels like SpeedBoats often operate with high-RPM engines, shifting their tonal energy to higher frequency bands and producing markedly different, high-intensity cavitation profiles. Submersibles like UUVs generate very subtle, high-frequency electric motor tonals, while the 'noise' class provides the ambient baseline against which these anthropogenic signatures must be detected.

To extract discriminative features from these complex, non-stationary acoustic signals, Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs) and Mel-spectrograms are universally employed in this domain. While originally developed for human speech recognition, these representations are highly effective in underwater acoustics. The Mel scale is a perceptual scale of pitches judged by listeners to be equal in distance from one another, which logarithmically spaces frequency bands. This characteristic effectively emphasizes lower frequencies, which is precisely where the majority of vessel acoustic energy and identifying tonal information reside. Mel-spectrograms provide a two-dimensional time-frequency representation that captures the temporal evolution of these frequency bands. MFCCs further decorrelate the Mel-filterbank energies via a Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT), yielding a compact, robust feature vector that effectively models the spectral envelope of the vessel's acoustic signature, rendering it invariant to certain channel-induced distortions and ambient noise fluctuations.

## 3. Formal Problem Definition

The objective of this project is mathematically formalized as a supervised multi-class classification problem. We define the core parameters of the system systematically to establish a rigorous framework for model development and evaluation.

Let the input space be denoted as $\mathcal{X}$. An individual instance $x \in \mathcal{X}$ represents a continuous underwater acoustic signal segment of a fixed duration $T$ seconds. This continuous signal $x(t)$ is discretized at a sampling frequency $f_s$, resulting in a one-dimensional discrete-time sequence of length $N = T \times f_s$. In practice, this raw acoustic waveform $x$ is not fed directly into the classifier but is mapped to a highly descriptive feature space $\mathcal{X}' \subset \mathbb{R}^{d \times \tau}$ (such as a Mel-spectrogram consisting of $d$ frequency bins and $\tau$ time frames) prior to classification.

Let the output space be denoted as $\mathcal{Y}$. This space represents the set of discrete, mutually exclusive categorical labels corresponding to the defined acoustic types. For the dataset utilized in this work, the output space is defined as $\mathcal{Y} = \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}$, where:
*   $0$ corresponds to the CargoShip class.
*   $1$ corresponds to the KaiYuan class.
*   $2$ corresponds to the noise class.
*   $3$ corresponds to the SpeedBoat class.
*   $4$ corresponds to the Uuv class.

The fundamental goal of the machine learning pipeline is to learn an optimal mapping function $f: \mathcal{X} \rightarrow \mathcal{Y}$ (or more accurately, $f: \mathcal{X}' \rightarrow \mathcal{Y}$ over the extracted feature space) parameterized by a set of learnable weights $\theta$. The function $f_\theta$ is designed to approximate the true underlying conditional probability distribution $P(y | x)$ of the acoustic signals. Specifically, the neural network or statistical model outputs a predicted probability vector $\hat{y} \in [0,1]^5$ such that the sum of its components $\sum_{i=1}^5 \hat{y}_i = 1$. The final predicted class label is then determined by the maximum likelihood estimate, mathematically expressed as $\text{argmax}_{i} (\hat{y}_i)$.

To quantitatively assess the generalization performance and predictive capability of the classification function $f_\theta$, several rigorous evaluation metrics are established:
*   **Accuracy:** The ratio of correctly predicted instances to the total number of instances evaluated in the dataset, providing a global, albeit sometimes limited, performance measure.
*   **F1-score:** The harmonic mean of Precision and Recall. This metric is critical in multi-class scenarios, particularly if inherent class imbalances exist within the dataset, as it provides a more robust evaluation than accuracy alone by penalizing both false positives and false negatives equivalently.
*   **AUC-ROC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve):** Extended for multi-class classification via One-vs-Rest (OvR) or One-vs-One (OvO) strategies, this metric evaluates the model's ability to discriminate between the classes across various decision threshold settings, reflecting the general robustness and confidence of the probabilistic predictions.

The optimization of the model parameters $\theta$ during the training phase is achieved by minimizing a designated loss function over a labeled training dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2), \dots, (x_m, y_m)\}$. Given the mutually exclusive multi-class nature of the problem, the Categorical Cross-Entropy Loss is the standard, theoretically sound objective function. The cross-entropy loss $\mathcal{L}$ for a single observation is mathematically defined as:

$$
\mathcal{L}(y, \hat{y}) = - \sum_{c=1}^{C} y_{c} \log(\hat{y}_{c})
$$

where:
*   $C$ denotes the total number of vessel classes (in this framework, $C=5$).
*   $y$ represents the ground-truth target vector, strictly encoded as a one-hot vector where $y_c = 1$ if the true class is indeed $c$, and $y_c = 0$ for all other classes.
*   $\hat{y}_{c}$ represents the model's predicted probability that the input acoustic segment $x$ belongs to class $c$.

Through stochastic gradient descent or related optimization algorithms, empirical risk minimization seeks to find the optimal parameter set $\theta^*$ that minimizes this expected cross-entropy loss over the entire empirical training distribution.
